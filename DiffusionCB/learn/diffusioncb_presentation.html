DiffusionCB

Can non‑Markovian noise be recovered from raw Cycle‑Benchmarking data?

Simulation‑based inference with diffusion models — a physics question, with machine learning as the instrument, not the goal.

01 — The Puzzle

A real anomaly we can't yet explain

Cycle Benchmarking on ibm_fez measured a two‑qubit decay far below what independent single‑qubit noise would predict.

λX = 0.90
independence baseline
λXX = 0.42
measured — dominant crosstalk

Standard CB collapses everything to that one decay constant λP, and by itself it cannot tell us why the gap exists:

a coherent over‑rotation,

a stochastic but memoryless (Markovian) correlated error, or

genuinely non‑Markovian noise — the environment carrying memory between cycles

λP alone cannot distinguish these three. This talk is about what can.

02 — Why Standard CB Fails Here

The theory assumes away exactly what we need

Standard RB/CB theory assumes Markovian noise: average sequence fidelity decays as one clean exponential, and the decay rate maps directly to gate infidelity.

Markovian assumption (standard RB / CB): F_seq(m) = A · p^m + B single exponential lambda_P = (F[m2] / F[m1])^(1 / (m2−m1)) Markovian, precisely (GKSL generator — what "no memory" means): Ṡ = −i[H,S] + sum_k gamma_k · ( L_k S L_k† − (1/2)L_k†L_k S − (1/2)S L_k†L_k ) one fixed generator, no history dependence Non‑Markovian reality: decay is generically NON‑exponential

Even where decay stays exponential, Brillant et al. (PRL 135, 070601) show the rate stops equaling gate infidelity under non‑Markovian, finite‑time‑gate noise. And the rigorous object here — the process tensor (Figueroa‑Romero et al., PRX Quantum 2, 040351) — gives a sharp, checkable criterion: its 2‑step Choi state Υ2:1 factorizes as Υ1:1⊗Υ1:1 exactly when the noise is Markovian.

Non‑factorizability of the process tensor's Choi state IS the non‑Markovian signature — not just "the decay looks bent."

03 — The Reframing

Turn it into an inference problem

Given a candidate noise structure, simulating what an experiment would look like is easy. Recovering that structure from real data is the actual, hard question.

Given raw (un‑averaged) CB data, infer the temporal‑correlation structure of the noise that produced it.

04 — Building a Controlled Testbed

Three noise classes, tuned to look identical to λP

A system qubit S is coupled to an environment qubit E each cycle. Tracing out and resetting E every step erases memory (Markovian); retaining E lets S‑E entanglement accumulate across cycles — that accumulation IS the memory.

shared per‑cycle Hamiltonian (S+E collision model): H = J·XSXE + hx·(XS+XE) + hy·(YS+YE) U_SE = exp(−i·delta·H)
non‑Markovian ("memory")

E carried forward across every cycle, traced out only at the end. This IS the memory.

stochastic‑Markovian ("jitter")

Same channel, but E is reset every cycle → i.i.d. noise, memoryless by construction.

coherent ("drift")

A fixed over‑rotation on S alone. No environment qubit at all.

All three calibrated to the same λP — so standard CB genuinely cannot tell them apart by construction.

One extra gate is the entire difference

Real circuits, straight from the harness — memory (left) vs jitter (right). Identical except for one thing: jitter inserts a reset (the grey |0〉 boxes) on the environment qubit after every U_SE.

Drift's circuit isn't pictured — it doesn't need to be: one qubit only, no environment, just twirl → cycle → RX(ε) repeated. No coupling gate to entangle with, nothing to reset.

06 — Result: Raw Data Separates What λ Cannot

Averaged, they look alike. Their spread doesn't.

At matched decay rate: average the raw data the way standard CB does, and memory can't be told apart from jitter. Look at the spread across sequences instead, and all three classes separate cleanly.

Same data, same classes, same matched decay rate. Averaging throws away exactly the part that tells them apart.

08 — Why Diffusion, Not Just a Classifier

A single answer hides uncertainty. A posterior reveals it.

A classifier gives one label. A Gaussian net gives one mean and one spread — always a single symmetric bump, even when two different values could explain the data equally well. What we actually want is a posterior: the full range of values the data is consistent with, not just one guess.

Classifier

One label — memory, jitter, or drift. Nothing about how strong, nothing about confidence.

Gaussian net

One mean, one spread. Forced into a single symmetric bump, even if two different δ values both fit.

Diffusion

Samples directly from the data. No fixed shape — if two answers are both plausible, it can draw two peaks instead of averaging into a fake middle value.

This is exactly why raw, un‑averaged CB data matters (Result, slide 06) — it's the input a posterior needs, and precisely what an average throws away.

11 — Where Diffusion's Inputs Come From

53 numbers in, one number out

Reminder: the Gaussian net's 36 features are just mean and variance, 3 Paulis × 6 lengths × 2. Diffusion reuses all 36 of those as its condition c — and adds two more pieces on top.

x_t = sqrt(alpha_bar_t) · x_0 + sqrt(1 − alpha_bar_t) · eps ↑↑↑↑↑↑↑↑↑↑↑↑↑↑↑ t is INSIDE alpha_bar_t — a different blend of signal and noise at every step. Given only x_t, the model can't recover which t produced it — so t must be fed in separately, alongside x_t.

THE 53 INPUTS, PIECE BY PIECE

  • x_t (1 number) — the noisy guess at this step, from the formula above
  • + time embedding of t (16 numbers) — t turned into 16 sin/cos values, so the network can read "which step" the way the formula's alpha_bar_t does
  • + c (36 numbers) — the same mean/variance features the Gaussian net uses
  • = 53 numbers, concatenated and fed in together

WHY THE OUTPUT DIFFERS TOO

The Gaussian net commits in one shot: one forward pass, out comes (μ,σ), done. Diffusion never predicts δ directly — it predicts the noise (eps_hat) added at this step, and the true δ only emerges after 100 steps of repeatedly subtracting that predicted noise. Different mechanisms, not just different numbers.

12 — What the Fit Actually Looks Like

Real predictions, real residuals, same test rows

Both from this run: predicted δ vs true δ (left), and how well the predicted uncertainty matches the errors (right, z‑score histogram against N(0,1)).

The Gaussian net has to pick one σ that works for every row — so the hard cases (the ambiguous δ≈0.2 band) blow past it, fattening the tails you can see in its histogram above. Diffusion's posterior width isn't fixed: tight where the data is unambiguous, wider exactly where it's genuinely uncertain — which is what pulls the outliers back in and tracks N(0,1).

14 — Scope

What this shows — and doesn't

10,000 simulated runs, non‑Markovian ("memory") class only, δ ∈ [0.02, 0.25], one seed=0 train/test split, one trained model per method.

Shown

  • On this controlled, matched comparison, diffusion's posterior shape fixed a real, measured overconfidence problem the Gaussian baseline had.
  • Point accuracy improved alongside calibration — not a trade‑off.

Not yet shown

  • Joint inference across noise classes + parameters
  • Generalization to a wider δ range
  • Real hardware data
  • Robustness across seeds/splits
15 — Next Steps

Where this earns its keep

Define the real inference target. Today's δ‑only regression is a 1‑D scaffold. The actual goal is joint inference — which mechanism, at what strength — where the posterior can be genuinely multi‑modal. A Gaussian net structurally can't represent that; diffusion can.

Candidate mechanisms, grounded in real transmon physics: two‑level‑system defects, classical 1/f drift, and qubit‑qubit crosstalk — already observed on real Fez hardware.

SPAM error isn't yet cancelled in the feature pipeline (today's simulated data has none) — needs explicit handling before real hardware features reach the model.

Real hardware, closing the loop: re‑run CB on ibm_fez retaining raw data, feed it to the trained model, and cross‑check its inference against the Brillant gate‑implementation diagnostic (ZSX vs U3 decay‑rate disagreement) — an independent test of whether the model's answer is right. This is what finally lets us explain the λXX = 0.42 anomaly we opened with.

Appendix A — How It Works

The diffusion model, briefly

forward (fixed, no learning): x_t = sqrt(alpha_bar_t)·x_0 + sqrt(1−alpha_bar_t)·eps learned (noise predictor): eps_hat = NoisePredictor(x_t, t, c) reverse mean (computed directly from the prediction): mu_theta(x_t,t) = (1/sqrt(alpha_t)) · ( x_t − ((1−alpha_t)/sqrt(1−alpha_bar_t)) · eps_hat )
  • Cosine noise schedule, T = 100 steps (Nichol & Dhariwal, 2021)
  • NoisePredictor: 3‑layer MLP, ~23,500 parameters, sinusoidal time embedding
  • Training: predict the injected noise (MSE loss); weights chosen by validation loss, to avoid overconfidence
  • Sampling: 100‑step reverse walk from pure noise, conditioned on a run's 36 features; repeated 1,000× per case
