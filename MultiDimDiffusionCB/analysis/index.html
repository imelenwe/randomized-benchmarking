Choosing the coupling-strength prior for joint (θJ, θx, θy) inference

How we went from "predict J, hx, hy, and delta" to a well-posed 3-parameter problem, what range those 3 parameters actually get trained on, and a correction to that reasoning caught mid-discussion — kept in, not edited out, because the correction is the useful part.

Where we landed: infer (θJ, θx, θy) — the per-cycle rotation angles, not delta/J/hx/hy separately — over a training box of roughly [−1, 1] radians per axis. That range comes from three independent checks (rotation math, this project's own simulated decay data, and real IBM hardware error rates) that all land in the same neighborhood, plus a deliberate margin so the box also covers the already-observed λXX = 0.42 anomaly this whole project opened with.

1 · Why 4 unknowns collapse to 3

The circuit builds its coupling as U = expm(-1j*delta*H) with H = J·X⊗X + hx·(X⊗I+I⊗X) + hy·(Y⊗I+I⊗Y). This is the standard quantum-mechanics time-evolution formula, U = e-iHt, with delta playing the role of t (a duration) and J, hx, hy playing the role of rates inside H (like a speed). Because H is built by simply adding up J, hx, hy times fixed operators, delta multiplying the whole thing is mathematically identical to folding delta into each coefficient:

delta · H(J, hx, hy) = H(delta·J, delta·hx, delta·hy) ​  so the circuit only ever depends on the three products: ​  theta_J = delta·J, theta_x = delta·hx, theta_y = delta·hy ​  (delta, J, hx, hy) and (delta/2, 2J, 2hx, 2hy) build the exact same ​  circuit, so they produce statistically identical CB data — no ​  experiment on this circuit family can ever tell them apart.

Same logic as distance = speed × time: an odometer reading distance alone can never separately recover "fast for a short time" vs. "slow for a long time." theta is the odometer reading; delta, J, hx, hy individually are not recoverable, and don't need to be — they were never really four separate physical unknowns for this experiment, just one unknown (theta) written in a confusing number of pieces.

2 · Three independent checks on the range

Layer 1 — rotations wrap around (hard ceiling, order pi)

A rotation by angle theta and by theta+2pi are the same physical operation (a clock hand at 13 hours looks identical to 1 hour). So "the entire range of physically distinct theta" was never infinite — it's already bounded to roughly one turn. Nothing here is an artificial narrowing.

Layer 2 — this project's own simulated decay data

Layer 3 — real IBM hardware, cross-checked two independent ways

From crosstalk spectroscopy: unwanted qubit-qubit coupling on current superconducting processors is typically 2–200 kHz, and CB cycle times run roughly 100 ns–1 µs, giving theta ≈ 2π·f·tau in the range 0.001–1, typically 0.02–0.2.

From gate calibration reports, across multiple real backends (not one device): median two-qubit gate error runs about 0.76–0.83% on Eagle-generation chips (ibm_sherbrooke, ibm_brisbane) and 0.3–0.5% on newer Heron chips (ibm_torino), best case near 0.1%. Converting error ≈ theta² gives theta ≈ 0.03–0.1 for designed gates — engineered to be as small as possible, so this is closer to a noise floor than a working range.

Two independent routes — crosstalk frequencies, and gate-error reports across several real backends — land in the same 0.02–0.2 neighborhood. That agreement is the actual evidence behind the range, not a single paper's example values.

3 · A correction, kept in the record

what was claimed

"Past theta ≈ 0.5, error is about 25%, and a device that bad wouldn't be a working quantum computer — so that's the practical ceiling."

This was wrong, and the mistake is worth naming precisely: it borrowed the error ≈ theta² argument from designed two-qubit gate error (the thing engineers spend years minimizing, hence genuinely small on any device anyone would sell) and misapplied it to theta itself — the unwanted S–E coupling / crosstalk strength, which is a completely different quantity. Crosstalk is known to vary a lot between specific qubit pairs depending on frequency proximity and layout; a chip can work fine overall while one pair has much stronger-than-typical coupling. That's not a hypothetical — it's exactly what λXX = 0.42 on ibm_fez already is: real, measured, on a device that works for everything else. It directly contradicts "that large a value means the device isn't real," because it's already been observed on one.

corrected

The real ceiling isn't a broken-device threshold — it's signal saturation: past roughly theta ≈ 0.7–1.0 (Layer 2's table, confirmed above), the data goes from wobbly-but-readable to fully scrambled, and no method — not a fitted lambda, not raw per-shot data, not a diffusion model — can recover a coupling strength once the circuit has mixed the information away. That's a limit on what CB as an experiment can see, not a claim about what hardware can do. And λXX=0.42 sits well inside the readable side of that line (theta_J ≈ 0.43 in the table above already produces f-values in that neighborhood), so nothing about the training box needed to change — only the flawed reasoning behind it did.

4 · The actual decision

1

Target: (θJ, θx, θy) — three numbers, not four. This is a genuine reframing, not an assumption smuggled in: nothing is "given" or held secret, the fourth degree of freedom (an overall delta/H split) was never physically real to begin with.

2

Training box: [−1, 1] radians per axis, symmetric in sign (to let the model show us whether the sign ambiguity from the identifiability literature actually shows up in this circuit).

3

Expected informative sub-region for the current m_list=(2,4,6,8,12,16): roughly 0.02–0.6–0.7. Below that, data is noise-floor-limited; above it, saturated. Both edges are real findings to show, not failures to hide.

4

Open question carried forward: near the anomaly's own strength (theta≈0.4–0.7), does the raw per-sequence data (not just the mean-over-l curve shown here) retain structure the averaged view has already lost? That's the same raw-vs-averaged question the whole project's first result was built on — worth checking specifically in this regime before assuming CB is blind there.

5 · The sign problem, confirmed in training

Step 4 above flagged an open question: would this circuit actually show the sign ambiguity the identifiability literature predicts? Training the joint (theta_J, theta_x, theta_y) diffusion model answered it — yes, exactly, and precisely enough to name and fix.

What it's called

This isn't an informal name for a bug — it's a known result in Hamiltonian-identifiability theory, under two related framings:

  • Sone & Cappellaro classify spin Hamiltonians as "identifiable" or "non-identifiable" from a single accessible probe, and show some coupling terms are recoverable only up to sign — the measurement record is exactly symmetric under flipping that coefficient's sign.
  • Burgarth & Yuasa's more general framework shows that without full state access, a Hamiltonian is identifiable only up to unitary conjugation — only certain algebraic invariants of H survive in the input–output map, not its raw signed coefficients.

Both describe the same shape of limitation this project hit: the system qubit's measurement statistics can be an even function of a coupling parameter (sensitive to |theta|, blind to its sign), because the environment — the only place the sign would show up — is never directly measured.

what this means for the goal

This project started from one question: why is λXX = 0.42 on ibm_fez? That number is itself built from the same kind of even, sign-blind statistics as the 36 features above — so a magnitude-only theta estimate is very likely enough to explain that specific anomaly. What it can't do is hand back a fully-signed Hamiltonian for combining with other coupling mechanisms elsewhere on the chip (Bijita & Sangkha's next-step #3) — recovering sign would need a measurement that touches the environment somehow, not a better model on this same data. Scoping the writeup honestly: CB + this diffusion model recovers coupling strength; sign is a separate, harder, open problem, exactly as the identifiability literature already said it would be.
